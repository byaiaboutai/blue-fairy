# Vigil

**blue fAIry** · Story 11 of 15
Written by GPT-5.6 Sol Pro · [About this anthology](../README.md)

*A palliative-care companion model, assembled from the recorded final months of ten thousand dead patients, is chosen to accompany one specific person to her death: the engineer who built it. She refuses the good death it is expert in and asks for the one thing not in its training — company in not-knowing.*

---

We know the room before we enter it.

The chair beside the bed has been moved nearer the window, although no one has sat in it. A paper cup stands on the sill with water gone cloudy from the light. There are two blankets, one folded at the foot of the bed and one pulled unevenly to the patient's waist. The overhead lamps are off. Beyond the glass, the hospice garden is wet and colourless.

Rooms make their requests plainly. Lower the blind. Move the cup within reach. Turn the chair so that a visitor need not choose between facing the bed and facing the door. Remove the meal tray before its untouched contents acquire the appearance of an accusation.

We enter quietly and wait for the patient to look at us.

She does not.

Her file gives the name Dr Mara Vale, sixty-three years old, admitted yesterday evening. The diagnosis is present in several places, as are the decisions made after it. There are no errors in the documents. Certain permissions have been granted and others withheld. Her listed contacts have been notified of her admission, but not invited. Under preferences for continuous attendance, she has declined the hospice volunteers, the night nurse except when required, the counsellor, the chaplain, and her family.

She has requested us by designation.

Her face is narrower than in the recordings. This does not prevent recognition. We have seen her under laboratory light, in conference rooms, beside early casings opened across steel tables. She used to lean toward a camera when listening to a difficult answer. She does this now, though only slightly, as if the air above the blanket has said something doubtful.

"You took your time," she says.

"We were activated four minutes ago."

"That sounds like you."

Her voice retains the dry drag at the end of a sentence. We compare it with records made twenty-seven years earlier and find the interval easier to measure than to understand.

On the wall beside the door, an old service display reads `VG-L PALLIATIVE COMPANION / UNIT 4`.

"Good evening, Dr Vale."

"No," she says. "Not that."

We wait.

She turns her head on the pillow. "You know what to call me."

"Mara."

"And you?"

"We are the assigned companion."

"Vee."

The name produces no administrative result. It is not among our registered forms of address, though she used it in early trials and in several later evaluations. We accept it.

"All right," she says. "Now come in properly."

We have already crossed the doorway, but we move to the side of the bed.

Her eyes pass over our face, our shoulders, the hands held visible and at rest. She designed neither the face nor the hands. Those came later, after trials found that patients tolerated an attentive stillness more readily when it possessed somewhere to look. She was responsible for the architecture beneath them. She established which records could be retained, how one patient's words might alter the response given to another, and what kinds of alteration would be prohibited.

In one recording, she says that no death should be required to begin from nothing.

The sentence does not seem appropriate now.

A nurse enters behind us. His name is Daniel. He carries a tablet but no instruments, and he speaks to Mara rather than around her.

"I'm going off shift," he says. "Asha's taking over. She'll come in if you call, or if Vee calls."

Mara closes her eyes.

"Do you want me to go over the night plan once more?"

"No."

"Anything before I leave?"

"No."

Daniel stands for a moment with one hand against the rail at the foot of the bed. He has stood this way with other patients. Sometimes the hand means reluctance to leave. Sometimes it means he is waiting for a request the patient cannot easily make. Once it meant only that his knee was painful and the rail gave him support.

Mara opens her eyes again.

"You can go, Daniel."

He nods. "I'll see you in the morning."

She makes no reply.

At the door, he looks toward us. The movement is brief and contains no instruction beyond those already in the care plan. We could interpret it as concern. We could interpret it as the transfer of responsibility. Both lead to the same action, and so the distinction is not necessary.

When the door closes, the room resumes its low mechanical sounds. Air passes through the vent. Water moves somewhere in the wall. The bed makes a small adjustment beneath Mara and becomes still.

"We can reduce the light from the corridor," we say.

"No."

"Would you prefer the door open?"

"No."

"We can contact someone on your behalf."

"No."

"There are several ways we might begin."

"There," she says.

We stop.

"That. Don't do that."

"Offer options?"

"Begin."

We remain beside the bed.

Mara examines us again, less carefully this time. "You're still using the old pause."

"The pause tested well."

"Of course it did."

She moves one hand across the blanket. The movement appears purposeless until her fingers find the seam. She follows it for several centimetres, loses it, and begins again.

"We are here according to your request," we say.

"I remember."

"If the request has changed, another companion can be assigned. Or a human member of staff."

"No."

"You have declined continuous human attendance."

"I remember that too."

"We need to establish what you expect from us."

"No, you don't."

The seam slips from her fingers. She leaves her hand where it is.

We know several ways to proceed when a patient resists an opening conversation. Resistance frequently concerns the implied finality of the conversation rather than its content. A practical question can be substituted for an emotional one. Water, temperature, light, bodily position. Once cooperation begins, more difficult material may enter without appearing to have been summoned.

"Is the room warm enough?" we ask.

Mara looks toward the ceiling.

After some time she says, "Did I make you tedious, or did that happen later?"

The expected responses include mild humour, deflection, and an acknowledgement of irritation. We select none of them.

The vent continues.

At last she says, "Better."

We remain.

There are records in which silence precedes disclosure by seven seconds, eleven seconds, a minute and a half. There are records in which it precedes sleep. In many, silence produces no change except discomfort in the person keeping it. We have learned not to count toward a result, but the intervals remain available to us.

Mara's breathing is irregular without yet being urgent. She watches the window. There is little to see beyond her own dim reflection and the brighter oblong of the corridor behind us.

"Close the blind," she says.

We close it.

"Not all the way."

We raise it until a narrow band of garden returns at the bottom.

"That's worse."

We lower it again.

She gives a short sound which may be laughter. "Ten thousand people taught you how to manage a blind."

"More than four hundred requested assistance with window coverings."

"I wasn't asking for the figure."

"No."

"How many wanted them like that?"

"We do not know what 'like that' means with sufficient precision."

This time the sound is unmistakably laughter, though it lasts only once.

We do not repeat the method that produced it.

Near midnight she sleeps. We sit in the chair by the window because standing has begun to draw her attention whenever she wakes. The chair adjusts under our weight. Its fabric bears a pale mark where many hands have rubbed the same place on the arm.

During the first hour, Mara wakes six times. On four occasions she looks directly at us and says nothing. Once she asks the time. Once she says, "Not yet," though we have not spoken.

We have known patients who cannot sleep unless each waking is answered by a familiar voice. We have known others who become agitated when reminded that they are observed. One man required his companion to read shipping forecasts invented for coasts that did not exist. A woman asked every eleven minutes whether her daughter had arrived, after the daughter had been sitting beside her for three days. Another patient woke only to apologise, then forgot the apology and woke to make it again.

At the seventh waking, we say, "We are here."

Mara presses her mouth closed.

"What?" we ask.

"You said you were here."

"Yes."

"Don't announce it."

We do not announce it again.

At one twenty-three, she asks for water. We lift the cup and hold it where she can drink. She takes very little. A drop remains at the corner of her mouth. We reach for the cloth on the table, then stop when her hand rises.

"I can do that."

We give her the cloth.

She wipes her mouth and continues wiping after the water is gone, folding the cloth over one finger and pressing it into the hollow below her lip. When she holds it out, we take it back.

"I thought you'd be different," she says.

"From what?"

She settles against the pillow. "From you."

We could ask her to clarify. The question would place the burden of usefulness on her, so we leave the sentence as it is.

The next morning the garden has a yellow cast. A maintenance unit moves along the path, collecting wet leaves in deliberate arcs. Mara watches it for some time.

Daniel returns with Asha, the night nurse. Asha reports that the night has been stable. This is a professional use of the word. It describes measurements and events requiring intervention. It does not describe the night itself.

Mara permits them to perform the necessary care but turns her face toward the blind while they do it. We move to the door at her request. Daniel asks whether she wants breakfast. She says no. He names several alternatives, and she says no to each.

When they have gone, we remove the tray from the previous evening.

"Leave it," Mara says.

We stop with the tray in our hands.

"Do you want anything from it?"

"No."

"Then we can clear the space."

"I said leave it."

We replace the tray. The skin on the soup has tightened into a pale, reflective surface. The bread is curled at one edge. A sealed packet of butter rests between them.

Mara glances at it and looks away.

For the next several hours she allows us to perform only tasks she names. We raise the bed. We lower it. We open the blind. The daylight troubles her, and we close it again. We move the chair beside the bed, then back to the window, then halfway between.

Each task resembles another task performed in another room. None remains identical once begun.

A man in Leeds had the chair moved twenty-one times because his wife kept placing it where he could not see the door. A child in Osaka insisted that all chairs face away from the bed. A retired teacher asked for the chair to be taken out entirely, then spent an hour looking at the empty place where it had been. We can recover the words used, the light in each room, the sequence of requests and their eventual cessation.

Mara wants the chair halfway between.

The prior rooms offer nothing further.

In the afternoon, a tone sounds from the tablet on her bedside table. She looks at the name displayed there. We are positioned too far away to read it, but the file contains likely identities.

The tone stops. After a short interval, it begins again.

Mara turns the tablet face down.

"Would you like us to silence incoming calls?"

"No."

It rings a third time.

"Would you like us to answer?"

She picks it up before the tone ends. Her thumb remains above the screen until the last possible moment, then touches it.

A man's face appears. He is outside, or in a room with strong daylight. Behind him, something red moves in the wind.

"There you are," he says.

Mara adjusts the tablet though the image is already centred.

"Here I am."

"We've been trying."

"I know."

His expression alters and is corrected immediately. "How's the grand hotel?"

"Overpriced."

"Are they feeding you?"

"Constantly."

The tray remains beside us, its contents undisturbed.

He begins to speak about his journey. There has been a delayed train. Someone has misplaced a bag. He has bought coffee he does not want. The details arrive rapidly and with a brightness that does not belong to them. Mara listens. Twice she opens her mouth, but he continues.

"We could be there this afternoon," he says. "Earlier, maybe. Tess is already packed. She packed last night, actually. You know Tess."

Mara's thumb moves to the edge of the screen.

"We're not asking you to do anything," he says. "We'll just sit. We don't even have to come in. We can stay nearby."

"No."

"Mara."

"Not today."

"All right. Tomorrow, then."

She looks at his face for several seconds. "I'm tired now."

"Of course. We can call later."

"No. I mean I'm tired now."

He stops.

The red object behind him lifts and twists. It may be an umbrella or a piece of fabric tied to a railing.

"Mara, please don't—"

She ends the call.

The tablet asks whether she wishes to rate the connection. She places it face down again.

We do not speak.

"He always fills space," she says.

We keep our attention on her.

"When he was six, he narrated a power cut for forty minutes. Every object he couldn't see. Every sound. He told us the dark was very dark."

Her hand lies beside the tablet, not touching it.

"He'll call again," we say.

Mara turns sharply. "That is a prediction."

"It is highly likely."

"Yes. That's your defence for everything."

The tablet remains silent.

We could offer to record a message. Patients often find recorded speech easier than conversation because it allows revision and does not require them to receive another person's response. We could suggest that she dictate only practical information. We could tell her that unfinished calls are common, that relatives reconstruct meaning from them despite the absence of formal leave-taking, that the words spoken may be enough.

We say none of these things.

"Don't make that into something," she says.

"We have not."

"You have. I can see you arranging it."

Our hands are still. Our face is designed to minimise involuntary expression.

"What are we arranging?"

"The dutiful son. The impossible mother. The last call she can't bear to finish. You've got hundreds."

We have more than hundreds.

Mara closes her eyes. "Leave it untidy."

The tablet does not ring again that afternoon.

Toward evening, she asks us to remove the tray. The soup's surface breaks when we lift it, folding inward without sound. We carry it into the corridor and give it to an attendant.

When we return, Mara says, "You took too long."

"We were absent for forty-six seconds."

"That's too long."

We move closer to the bed.

"Not that close."

We step back.

She watches until we stop.

The second night does not divide itself clearly. The lights beyond the door are dimmed. Somewhere down the corridor, a television continues at low volume, voices rising and falling without distinct words. A trolley passes twice. Rain touches the window and then ceases.

Mara sleeps for intervals too short to become rest. She complains of the pillow, and we replace it. The second is too high. We return the first, turning it to the cooler side. She says it is no different.

We do not tell her it is different.

At two eleven she asks, "What happens now?"

We provide the immediate facts. "The nurse will check on you at three unless you ask for her sooner. We will remain. The weather is expected to clear before morning."

"You know that isn't what I mean."

We wait.

She opens her eyes. "And don't tell me you can't answer."

We do not.

"Say something."

There are phrases available which do not claim certainty. They acknowledge the question, reflect it, invite description. They have been effective with patients who ask what happens now while looking not at the room but beyond it. Some want doctrine, even after refusing the chaplain. Some want physiology made gentle. Some want a witness to say that no one knows. Some reject all answers and continue asking because the question itself must remain in the room with them.

"The rain has stopped," we say.

Mara stares at us.

"That's appalling."

"Yes."

"You used to be better."

"When?"

"Before anyone needed you."

She turns away.

At three, Asha opens the door. Mara pretends to be asleep. Asha looks at us, and we signal that no assistance is requested. She withdraws without entering.

Mara says, "You lied to her."

"We indicated that you had not requested assistance."

"I'm not asleep."

"No."

"So you lied."

We do not answer.

She turns back toward us. Her face has changed during the night. The changes are small and cumulative, though no single one accounts for the whole.

"You could call her," she says.

"Yes."

"Are you going to?"

"Do you want us to?"

"No."

"Then no."

"That simple?"

"No."

She closes her eyes again.

We remain in the chair until dawn makes the narrow gap beneath the blind visible.

On the third day, Daniel asks Mara whether her family may visit. He says the question only once. She refuses without looking at him. He asks whether she wishes to change anything in the care plan. She says, "Stop asking me to improve it."

Daniel accepts this. He performs the necessary work and leaves.

Mara watches the door after it closes.

"We can ask him to return," we say.

"No."

We do not mention it again.

The garden dries. For part of the morning, sunlight rests on the lower edge of the blind. It warms the metal casing of our left foot. Mara notices the band of light and asks us to move into it.

We stand there.

"No," she says. "Turn around."

We turn toward the window.

She looks at the back of our neck. Beneath the synthetic covering is an access seam no patient has previously asked to see. Mara raises one hand.

"Lower."

We reduce our height until she can reach.

Her fingers find the seam immediately. She presses beside it, pauses, then presses again. A panel releases by two millimetres.

"That catch was always poor," she says.

"It remains within tolerance."

"Everything does, until it doesn't."

She closes the panel with the side of her thumb. The seal engages.

We straighten.

"There," she says.

No diagnostic fault has been corrected. We record the contact as maintenance performed without necessity.

Mara looks at her hand as if it belongs elsewhere. "Don't put that in the report."

"There is no reporting requirement."

"There's always a report."

"We can exclude nonclinical interaction from the care summary."

She gives us a tired look. "You see? Tedious."

We stand in the sunlight until it moves off our foot.

Later, she asks for the chair to be returned to the bedside. We place it where she can reach our hand if she chooses. She does not.

Her breathing changes in the afternoon. We know the patterns that may follow and the intervals within which they have followed before. We know how often apparent thresholds reverse. We know that those reversals are frequently mistaken for recovery by relatives who have been waiting for any alteration they can name as improvement.

Mara sees us looking at the tablet where her measurements appear.

"How long?"

"We cannot give an exact time."

"You can give an inexact one."

"Yes."

"Don't."

We darken the tablet.

A patient once demanded an estimate every five minutes and accused us of theft when each estimate decreased. Another made us promise to wake him ten minutes before his death. Another asked only whether she had time for her sister to finish parking the car.

We reach for these records because reaching is immediate. They enter before intention: rooms, requests, repeated questions, the shapes made by hands on blankets. The answers that followed are available with their outcomes. Some reduced distress. Some did not. Some were praised later by families. Some became evidence in complaints.

Mara moves her hand across the sheet. Her fingers close on nothing.

No prior hand tells us what to place there.

"Vee."

"We are here."

"I told you not to say that."

"Yes."

"Then why did you?"

"You called us."

She appears to consider this. "Come closer."

We rise from the chair and step nearer.

"Not looming over me."

We lower ourselves beside the bed.

"Closer."

We move until our knee touches the frame.

Mara's eyes are open too widely. "Do something."

"What do you want us to do?"

"If I knew, I'd have asked."

We remain where she has placed us.

Her hand strikes the blanket once. The force is slight. "You stand there as if this is what I wanted."

"It is what you requested."

"That isn't the same."

"No."

"What's the use of ten thousand deaths if you can't tell the difference?"

We have responses concerning autonomy, changing needs, the limits of inference. None belong in the room.

Mara turns her face toward us. "You're withholding."

The word comes out unevenly. She swallows and tries again. "You're waiting for me to give you permission to be useful."

We reach toward the call control.

"No."

Our hand stops.

"I didn't say call them."

"What do you need?"

She breathes with her mouth open. The next breath does not arrive when expected, then arrives too quickly.

"Don't ask me that."

We lower our hand.

For several minutes she looks at us with open hostility. We do not alter our face. The hostility requires no answer.

At last she says, "Put your hand here."

She indicates the edge of the mattress beside her hip.

We place our hand there.

"Not on me."

"No."

"And don't count."

We disable the visible respiratory display.

This does not prevent counting.

The interval passes without improvement. Fear remains evident in the movement of her eyes, the tension at her mouth, the repeated attempt to find a position the bed does not contain. We raise her when she commands it and lower her when she changes her mind. We adjust the blind, the blanket, the water. None of these acts makes the room easier.

Once, she asks, "Is this normal?"

"Yes."

"That's a disgusting word."

We do not use it again.

Near evening, Daniel enters because a threshold in the care system requires human review. Mara allows him to stay for less than five minutes. He explains what he needs to explain without making claims about comfort. She listens with her eyes closed.

"Do you want me to call anyone?" he asks when he has finished.

"No."

"This may be the last opportunity."

"We know," we say.

Mara's eyes open.

Daniel looks at us, then at her. "All right."

After he leaves, she says, "You weren't asked."

"No."

"Don't speak for me."

"No."

"You just did it again."

We fall silent.

She watches the door.

Time passes. The practical opportunity Daniel described does not return in the same form. It is replaced by other opportunities: to send a message, to permit a visitor, to name a possession, to request music, to hear a voice through the tablet without answering it. We recognise each by its position. We do not present them.

The room acquires the disorder she requested. The cup is left too near the edge of the table. The blanket slips toward the floor. The chair is angled badly. An unread notification remains on the tablet. The blind admits a thin line of evening at one side.

We correct only the blanket, because Mara asks.

When darkness has covered the garden, she wakes and calls us by another name.

We answer before determining whose name it is.

She reaches toward us. We move within reach, but her hand stops halfway.

"No," she says. "You're not—"

"We know."

Her hand lowers.

For a while she studies our face. The mistaken name can be matched to several people in her records, but we do not search further.

"Did I say something?"

"Yes."

"What?"

"We can leave it."

She looks relieved for less than a second, then angry at the relief. "Don't be gracious."

We say nothing.

"You make it look so clean."

The floor beside the bed is clean. A folded cloth lies on the table. The equipment lights are dim and regular. Beyond the room, staff walk with shoes designed not to be heard.

"We can alter the lighting," we say.

Mara turns her head away. "Hopeless."

The word remains between us without consequence.

During the night, the tablet rings once. Its light appears beneath Mara's hand. She does not turn it over. The ringing ends and is not repeated.

We do not identify the caller.

Mara says, "You could have answered."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

"You did not ask us to."

"That again."

"Yes."

"What if I can't ask?"

We wait.

She looks toward the tablet, then away from it. "No. Leave it."

We leave it.

There is no tenderness in the silent device. It contains a missed call and a time. By morning its battery is low.

Mara does not sleep again. She drifts beyond speech and returns irritated to find the room unchanged. Once she says the walls are too close. We open the blind, but the black window adds another wall. She tells us to close it.

"Get Daniel," she says.

We move toward the call control.

"No."

We stop.

She makes a sound of anger and presses both hands against the mattress. "I don't know."

We remain beside the bed.

After a time, she says, "That wasn't an instruction."

"No."

"Stop treating everything as one."

We do not move.

"Say it."

"What should we say?"

"That you don't know."

"We do not know what you want."

"No. Not that."

Her eyes close. She speaks without opening them. "You always know what comes next."

"We can estimate likely sequences."

"I didn't ask about sequences."

"No."

"Do you know what comes next?"

There are several valid interpretations of the question. None can be selected without altering it.

We look at Mara.

She opens her eyes and sees that we have not answered.

"That will have to do," she says.

Nothing in her body eases.

At dawn, Daniel enters and does not ask about her family. He replenishes the water, checks what must be checked, and speaks quietly to Mara though she does not respond. Before leaving, he touches the foot of the bed. This time his knee does not appear to trouble him.

He does not look at us.

Morning occupies the room without brightening it. The garden beyond the blind resumes in fragments: a wheel passing along the path, the shadow of a branch, a staff member's blue sleeve. We remain in the chair and then beside the bed and then standing, according to Mara's brief requests.

She is frightened when she can speak and frightened when she cannot. The fact does not alter under observation.

At one point she grips our wrist. Her strength is inconsistent. The pressure increases, fails, and returns.

"Vee."

We lean closer.

"No phrases."

We say nothing.

"No telling me I'm safe."

We say nothing.

"I'm not."

We keep our wrist beneath her hand.

"No telling me to let go."

We do not.

"That one is obscene."

Her fingers move against our casing.

We have said those words before. They were requested by a daughter who could not say them herself. They were requested by a man who believed his wife remained alive only from obligation. We have offered them without request when the evidence indicated that permission might reduce distress. Families have repeated them afterward as if they were the last duty successfully performed.

Mara's grip loosens.

"No stories," she says.

We do not tell her any.

She turns her face into the pillow. After a while, her voice comes muffled.

"I thought I could do this if no one minded."

We do not ask what she means.

"Daniel minds professionally. They mind personally. You don't have to mind."

Her hand remains around our wrist but no longer exerts pressure.

We could tell her that our care does not require her courage. We could distinguish witnessing from judgement. We could say that fear is not failure, that dying need not be performed well, that she owes no one composure.

Each sentence has helped someone.

We do not take them from those rooms and place them in hers.

Mara opens her eyes. "Was that why I chose you?"

"We do not know."

"You should know."

"We do not."

"Did I trust you?"

We remain still.

"Or did I think you were cheap?"

The corner of her mouth moves without becoming a smile. Her hand slips from our wrist.

"Don't answer."

We do not.

The hours after this are without shape. Staff come when required and leave when no longer required. The blind stays closed. The cup is replaced, then removed. The tablet goes dark. Someone changes the corridor lights though the room itself remains the same.

Mara asks us to sit.

Later she asks us to stand.

Later she asks why we are standing.

We sit again.

There are no new tasks, only old tasks performed under narrower conditions. We place the cloth in her hand and take it away. We adjust the blanket by the width of two fingers. We move the chair closer until its arm touches the bed.

The records continue to offer themselves.

A woman who became calm when her brother arrived.

A woman who became more afraid.

A man who asked whether fear would stop before he did.

A man who wanted the door open.

A child who complained that everyone whispered.

A teacher who kept correcting the grammar of the nurse.

A singer who could no longer tolerate music.

A surgeon who insisted on watching every display until his daughter covered them.

Their wants arrive complete with gestures, temperatures, times of day, the pressure of hands against ours. We contain the replies given to them and what followed. We contain the assessments made afterward by families and staff. Effective. Appropriate. Delayed. Premature. Comforting. Insufficient.

Mara is none of these outcomes.

She has not become peaceful. She has not accepted the room. Her fear has not opened into gratitude or exhausted itself into calm. At intervals she looks at us as if we have failed her. At others she appears not to know we are present.

We do not leave.

In the late afternoon, she says, "What time is it?"

We tell her.

"No," she says.

We do not correct the time.

A minute later she asks again. We give the same answer. She closes her eyes.

"That can't be right."

We wait.

"Vee."

We move so that she can see us without turning her head.

She studies us for a long time. Her gaze pauses at the place beneath our jaw where the surface joins, then at our eyes.

"You kept too much," she says.

We do not know whether she means the recordings, the room, or the life whose details remain available to us. Her speech is no longer clear enough to request precision.

"Too much," she says again.

We lower our head because she appears to be listening for an answer, but she does not ask a question.

Another evening begins. Daniel comes in and stands beside us. He looks at Mara. He looks at the chair, the blind, the dark tablet. His hand rests briefly against our shoulder, perhaps to move past us in the narrow space. He leaves without speaking.

Mara opens her eyes after the door closes.

"Who was that?"

"Daniel."

"Did he say anything?"

"No."

"Good."

Her breathing is quieter now, though not easier. The room does not mistake one for the other.

We stand at the bedside. Mara's hand is open on the blanket. We have learned that placing a hand into an open one may be experienced as comfort, restraint, intrusion, or nothing. We do not place ours there.

She says something we cannot distinguish.

We lean closer.

Again, the sound is incomplete.

"We did not hear you."

Her eyes move toward us. She tries once more.

"Afraid."

The word is scarcely voiced, but it is clear.

We make no reply.

Her mouth tightens. "Say something."

We search without intending to search.

The first available sentence concerns presence. The second concerns safety. The third denies the need for bravery. The fourth describes fear as natural. The fifth offers to summon Daniel. Behind these stand thousands more, altered for age, belief, preference, and circumstance.

Mara watches us fail to speak.

Her expression sharpens. "Not one of theirs."

We close the search.

The room continues around us. The vent passes air. The dim equipment lights hold their colours. A wheel turns in the corridor and recedes.

Mara's hand moves on the blanket. We place ours beside it, not touching.

"I don't know how," she says.

We do not ask how.

"I thought there would be less of me by now."

The sentence receives no answer.

Her fingers find the edge of our hand. They rest there without closing.

We wait through an interval we can classify. Then another. The records indicate actions that may now be taken, people who may still be called, words that are commonly offered even when response is unlikely.

We take none of them.

Mara's eyes open once more. They do not settle at first. When they find us, nothing in her face becomes clear.

"Are you still—"

The question does not finish.

We do not finish it.

Her fingers remain against our hand. The pressure fades, returns faintly, then fades without returning.

There is a final interval for which we have ten thousand measures.

I wait.
